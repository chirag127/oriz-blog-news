Media literacy is not a school subject you passed once. It is a daily skill, and the environment it operates in keeps changing. A generation ago the risk was a biased newspaper. Today the risk is an information stream tuned to your attention, filled with content that is cheap to fake and expensive to check. The basics below are the minimum toolkit for reading that stream without being played by it.

## The feed is not the world

The most important thing to understand about modern media is that your feed is not a neutral window onto events. It is a ranked, personalized product, ordered by a system whose job is to keep you engaged — not to keep you informed. That distinction shapes everything you see.

Two consequences follow:

- **You are seeing a sample, chosen for reaction.** The stories that reach you are disproportionately the ones that provoke — because provocation drives engagement, and engagement is what the system optimizes. The calm, important, complicated story loses to the loud, simple, infuriating one every time.
- **Two people can inhabit different realities.** Your feed and someone else's are assembled from different signals. When you assume everyone saw what you saw, you misjudge how the world actually looks to others. Part of literacy is remembering that your stream is *yours*, not *the* news.

The practical takeaway: treat your feed as a curated sample with a bias toward outrage, and go looking for what it is not showing you.

## Headlines are written to be clicked, not to inform

A headline has one job in an attention economy: earn the click. That is a different job from summarizing the article accurately, and the two often conflict. This is why headlines overstate, why they pose questions the article answers with "no," and why the body of a piece is frequently more measured than its title.

Simple defenses:

- **Read past the headline before reacting.** The correction, the caveat, and the "actually it's complicated" almost always live in paragraph four, not the title.
- **Beware the question headline.** "Is this common food giving you cancer?" The answer, buried below, is nearly always "no, not really." A headline phrased as a question is a signal the evidence could not support a statement.
- **Notice the missing subject.** "Officials linked to scandal" — which officials, linked by whom, how? Vague headlines are vague on purpose when the specifics would deflate them.

## Check the image before you trust it

Images and video feel like proof, which is exactly why they are the sharpest tool for deception. A real photo shown in the wrong context does more damage than an obvious fake, because it survives scrutiny. Three quick checks catch most of it:

1. **Reverse image search.** Drop a suspicious photo into a reverse image search. If it turns out to be years old or from a different country, you have found the deception in seconds.
2. **Look for the edges of the frame.** Cropping is the cheapest manipulation there is. Ask what might be just outside the shot — the counter-protester, the context, the thing that changes the meaning.
3. **Distrust the too-perfect.** As synthetic images improve, the tell is often that a scene is *too* aligned with a narrative — perfectly composed, perfectly on-message. Real events are messier than the images that go viral about them.

## Understand how misinformation spreads

Bad information rarely spreads because people are stupid. It spreads because it is engineered to be shareable — emotionally charged, simple, and flattering to a group's existing beliefs. Recognizing the machinery helps you resist it:

- **It targets identity, not logic.** The most viral falsehoods confirm what a group already wants to believe. If a claim makes your side look good and the other side look evil with suspicious neatness, slow down.
- **It travels faster than the correction.** By the time a false claim is debunked, it has usually reached far more people than the correction ever will. This is why not sharing something unverified matters — you are a link in the chain either way.
- **Repetition feels like truth.** The more often you see a claim, the more true it feels, regardless of evidence. Seeing something five times is not five pieces of proof; it is often one piece of misinformation, amplified.

## Build a deliberate information diet

Literacy is not only defense; it is design. Left to default settings, your information diet is chosen for you by systems optimizing for attention. Choosing it yourself is the highest-leverage habit here:

- **Go directly to a few trusted sources** rather than accepting whatever the algorithm serves. A handful of outlets and writers you have vetted beats an endless, unvetted stream.
- **Read across the disagreement.** Deliberately read a careful source you tend to disagree with. Not to be persuaded, but to understand the strongest version of the other case — and to notice when your own side is spinning.
- **Leave room for "I don't know yet."** The pressure to have an instant opinion on every event is itself a distortion. On a breaking story, the literate position is often to wait. The first reports are usually wrong in the details, and there is no penalty for forming your view a day late and getting it right.

None of this requires you to be cynical or to trust nothing. The goal is the opposite: to spend your trust deliberately, on sources and claims that have earned it, instead of having it extracted from you by whatever is loudest today.
