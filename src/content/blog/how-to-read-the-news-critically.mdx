Most of us read the news the way we scroll a feed: fast, once, and looking for the point. That is exactly how a weak story slips past you. Reading critically is not about doubting everything. It is a short, repeatable method you run on any story before you decide whether to believe it, share it, or act on it.

Here is the method this desk uses. It takes about two minutes per story and gets faster with practice.

## Separate the claim from the proof

Every story is a claim before it is a fact. The claim is what the headline says happened. The proof is what the story shows to back it up. These are two different things, and good reporting keeps them apart on purpose.

When you read, physically split them in your head:

- **The claim:** "New policy will cut prices by 30%."
- **The proof offered:** a government press release, a named economist, a leaked memo, or nothing at all.

If the proof is only "sources say" with no name, no document, and no way to check, you are reading a claim wearing the costume of a fact. That does not make it false. It makes it *unverified* — and you should hold it lightly until more arrives.

## Find the primary source

News is a chain. A tweet quotes a blog that summarizes an article that paraphrases a study that surveyed 40 people. By the time it reaches you, the number has usually grown and the caveats have fallen off.

Walk back up the chain until you hit the primary source — the original study, the full transcript, the actual filing, the person who was there. Two habits make this easy:

1. **Follow the link.** If a story cites a report, open the report. The gap between what the report says and what the story says about it is often the whole story.
2. **Search the quote.** Paste a striking quote into a search engine in quotation marks. You will find where it first appeared and whether it was trimmed to change its meaning.

> **The two-source rule.** Do not treat a claim as settled until you have seen it confirmed by two independent sources — not two outlets copying the same wire story, but two reporters who did their own work.

## Ask what is missing

A story is shaped as much by what it leaves out as by what it includes. Trained readers ask three questions on every piece:

- **Who is not quoted?** A story about a factory closing that quotes only the company, never a worker, is telling half the story on purpose.
- **What is the denominator?** "Crime up 50%" means nothing without the base. Fifty percent of two incidents is three. Fifty percent of two thousand is a crisis. Always ask "out of how many?"
- **What happened before the clip started?** Video is powerful and easy to crop. The thirty seconds you were shown may have been chosen to remove the thirty seconds that explain it.

## Check the timing and the incentive

Two context questions catch most manipulation:

**When did this happen?** Old stories get recirculated as if they are new, especially during a crisis. Check the publication date, not the date it appeared in your feed. A three-year-old photo presented as today's event is one of the most common forms of misinformation, and it takes five seconds to catch.

**Who benefits from you believing this?** Every story has a source, and sources have interests. A drug's benefits are often reported by a press release from the company that sells it. A political scandal is often surfaced by the opponent it damages. Knowing the incentive does not tell you the story is false — it tells you where to point your skepticism.

## Read the correction culture

You can judge an outlet by how it handles being wrong. Serious newsrooms correct errors openly, date the correction, and explain what changed. Outlets that quietly edit or never admit mistakes are telling you they optimize for looking right, not being right. Before you trust a source repeatedly, look for its corrections page. Its absence is information.

## Slow down on the stories that make you angry

The stories engineered to spread are the ones that trigger a strong emotion — outrage, fear, vindication. That reaction is the point; it is what makes you share before you check. The discipline is simple and hard: **the angrier a story makes you, the more you should slow down.** Run the whole method on it before you pass it on. The stories most worth checking are exactly the ones you least want to.

## The two-minute checklist

Keep this somewhere you will see it:

1. What is the **claim**, and what **proof** is offered?
2. Can I reach the **primary source**?
3. Who is **not quoted**, and what is the **denominator**?
4. **When** did this happen, and **who benefits** from my believing it?
5. Does this outlet **correct** its mistakes?
6. Am I reacting to the facts, or to the **feeling**?

None of this requires expertise. It requires the willingness to spend two minutes before you decide. In an information environment built to make you decide instantly, that pause is the whole skill.
